Snakebite first aid in the USA and Canada

Know what to do before you ever need it.

A venomous snakebite is a medical emergency. In the United States, about 7,000 to 8,000 people are bitten by venomous snakes each year and around five of them die, according to the CDC. Most deaths are avoided because people reach hospital quickly for antivenom. If someone has been bitten, call 911 and go to an emergency room straight away. In the USA you can also call Poison Control on 1‑800‑222‑1222; in Canada, call 911 or your provincial poison centre.

First aid for a snakebite

1

Call 911

Call 911 (in the USA and Canada) or get to an emergency room at once, even if the bite does not hurt much yet. Note the time of the bite. In the USA, Poison Control (1‑800‑222‑1222) can also advise you and the hospital.

2

Keep still and calm

Sit or lie down and keep the bitten limb still. Moving around spreads venom faster. Do not walk out if help can come to you, and keep away from the snake.

3

Take things off

Remove rings, watches, bracelets and tight clothing or boots near the bite before swelling starts. Cover the bite loosely with a clean, dry dressing.

4

Watch and report

Hospitals in the USA and Canada keep antivenom for rattlesnake, copperhead and cottonmouth bites. Tell the doctors about any swelling, bruising, numbness or tingling, a metallic taste, drooping eyelids or trouble breathing. Coral snake bites can look mild for hours before they get serious.

What NOT to do

  • Do not use a tourniquet, and do not wrap the limb tightly. For rattlesnake and other pit viper bites a tight or pressure bandage can make the damage worse.
  • Do not cut the bite or try to suck out the venom, by mouth or with a suction kit.
  • Do not put ice or cold packs on the bite, and do not soak it in water.
  • Do not drink alcohol or caffeine, and do not take pain medicine such as aspirin or ibuprofen unless a doctor says so.
  • Do not try to catch or kill the snake. A photo from a safe distance is enough, or none at all.
  • If the person is unconscious, lay them on their side (recovery position) and keep the airway clear. Do not give anything by mouth.

Avoiding bites

  • Watch where you step and where you put your hands: around rocks, logs, woodpiles and ledges, and in tall grass. Stay on trails.
  • Wear boots and long trousers in snake country, and carry a light on warm nights, when many snakes are about.
  • Step over logs onto the far side only when you can see it, and never reach under rocks or into holes.
  • Leave every snake alone. If you meet one, step back slowly and give it room. Many bites happen when people try to handle or kill a snake.
  • Keep yards free of rubbish and wood piles that shelter rodents, and keep children and pets away from any snake.

The venomous snakesPit vipers and coral snakes

Almost every venomous bite in the USA and Canada is from a pit viper: a rattlesnake, a copperhead or a cottonmouth. Coral snakes live in the southern states and bite rarely. Learn to recognise them, but treat every bite as an emergency.

Myths and facts

MythRattlesnakes always rattle before they strike.

FactNot always. A rattlesnake that feels hidden often stays silent, and young snakes have only a small button. Watch where you put your hands and feet.

Myth"Red touches yellow, kills a fellow" tells you which snakes are safe.

FactDo not trust it. The rhyme only fits coral snakes in the United States, odd-coloured individuals break it, and it does not work south of the border. Leave any banded snake alone.

MythA baby rattlesnake is more dangerous than an adult.

FactA myth. Adults carry far more venom. Any venomous snake of any size deserves respect.

MythYou can tell a rattlesnake’s age from its rattle.

FactNo. A new segment is added each time the skin is shed, which can be several times a year, and segments break off.

MythCottonmouths cannot bite under water.

FactThey can. Give any snake in or near water plenty of room.

MythCutting the bite and sucking out the venom, or a suction kit, works.

FactIt does not. Suction devices remove almost no venom, and cutting causes bleeding and infection while wasting time.

MythA tourniquet or ice keeps the venom from spreading.

FactBoth make things worse. They trap venom in the limb and add to the tissue damage. Get to an emergency room instead.

MythA dead snake is safe.

FactNot always. Reflexes can make a freshly killed snake (even a severed head) bite. Do not handle dead snakes with bare hands.

MythSnakes chase people.

FactSnakes flee when they can. Many bites in North America happen when someone tries to catch, move or kill a snake.

This page is general information and not medical advice. It follows the advice of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (NIOSH) and current emergency-medicine practice for pit viper and coral snake bites. Follow the advice of local doctors, Poison Control and emergency services.
