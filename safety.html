06Safety

Snakebite first aid in India

Know what to do before you ever need it.

A snakebite is always a medical emergency. Research estimates that snakebite caused about 58,000 deaths a year in India between 2000 and 2019, mostly among rural people, and most of these deaths could have been prevented by getting to hospital quickly for anti-snake venom. If someone has been bitten, call 108 (ambulance) or 112 and head for a hospital straight away.

First aid: do it R.I.G.H.T.

R

Reassure

Keep the person calm and still. Fear and panic speed up the spread of venom. Many bites are from harmless snakes, and even venomous bites can be treated successfully if the person reaches hospital in time.

I

Immobilise

Hold the bitten limb still, like a fractured limb, with a splint or sling. Remove rings, bangles, watches and anklets before swelling starts. Avoid walking if you can. Carry or transport them.

GH

Get to Hospital immediately

Go to the nearest hospital that stocks anti-snake venom, by the fastest transport available, even if there are no symptoms yet. Kraits in particular can bite with little pain or swelling, and paralysis can follow hours later.

T

Tell the doctor

Report anything that develops on the way: drooping eyelids, blurred vision, trouble speaking or breathing, vomiting, bleeding or spreading swelling, and the time of the bite. Describe the snake or show a photo if one was taken safely.

What NOT to do

  • Do not tie a tight band, rope or tourniquet around the limb.
  • Do not cut the bite, burn it, or try to suck out venom.
  • Do not apply ice, herbs, chemicals, cow dung or any home remedy.
  • Do not give alcohol, and do not wait for symptoms or for a traditional healer.
  • Do not chase, catch or kill the snake. It wastes time and risks more bites.
  • If the person is unconscious, lay them on their side (recovery position) and keep the airway clear. Do not give anything by mouth.

Avoiding bites

  • Carry a torch and wear closed shoes or boots when walking outdoors at night or in tall grass.
  • Sleep off the floor on a cot, and under a bed-net where kraits are common. Kraits often enter homes at night.
  • Never put hands into holes, woodpiles, rubble or thick vegetation. Use a stick to check first.
  • Keep the area around the house clear of rubbish and rodents, which attract snakes.
  • If you meet a snake, stop, step back slowly and give it space. Keep children and pets away and call a trained snake rescuer or the forest department.

The "Big Four"Cause most snakebite deaths in India

The standard Indian polyvalent anti-snake venom is made against these four. Learn to recognise them, but remember that any bite should be treated as an emergency.

Indian CobraNaja naja
Common KraitBungarus caeruleus
Russell's ViperDaboia russelii

Myths and facts

MythVenomous snakes have triangular heads and harmless ones do not.

FactUnreliable. Cobras and kraits, two of the deadliest groups in India, have narrow, rounded heads, while many harmless snakes can flatten their heads into a triangle when threatened.

MythRound pupils mean a snake is harmless.

FactWrong. Cobras, kraits and king cobras all have round pupils. Never judge danger from a photo or a quick glance.

MythSnakes drink milk and love it.

FactNo. Snakes are reptiles that eat whole animals. Milk cannot be digested and offering it harms the snake.

MythSnakes chase people and take revenge.

FactSnakes flee when they can. Almost all bites happen when a snake is stepped on, cornered, handled or attacked.

MythSnake charmers’ music makes snakes dance.

FactSnakes do not respond to tunes. They sway following the moving pipe. Snake charming is illegal in India because the snakes are cruelly treated.

MythCutting the bite and sucking out the venom works.

FactIt does not. It increases infection and bleeding and wastes precious time. Tight bandages and tourniquets can also cause lasting damage.

MythHerbs, stones, mantras or a healer can cure a bite.

FactOnly anti-snake venom given in hospital treats envenoming. Delays for traditional treatment cost lives.

MythA baby snake is more dangerous because it cannot control its venom.

FactA myth. Adult snakes carry more venom. Any venomous snake of any size deserves respect.

MythA dead snake is safe.

FactNot always. Reflexes can make a freshly killed snake (even a severed head) bite. Do not handle dead snakes with bare hands.

This page is general information and not medical advice. Guidance follows the widely used "Do it R.I.G.H.T." approach in the national and WHO South-East Asia snakebite guidelines. Follow the advice of local doctors and emergency services.
